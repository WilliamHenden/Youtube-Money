    How Norway Saved $2.3 Trillion and Britain Saved Nothing

    Draft 1 · about 1,200 words · times are the plan, not measured

    Hook0:00 to 0:25

    In nineteen sixty-five, Britain and Norway drew a line down the middle of the North Sea and agreed that whatever lay under the seabed on each side belonged to them. A few years later, both of them found oil.

    Today, Norway's side of that line has turned into a fund worth around two point three trillion dollars. Britain's side has turned into no fund at all.

    They had the same sea and the same oil. So what did Norway do that Britain didn't?

    Context0:25 to 1:20

    To understand it, you have to go back to a time when nobody thought Norway had any oil. Through the nineteen sixties, companies drilled well after well off the Norwegian coast and found almost nothing worth having.

    By nineteen sixty-nine, the American company Phillips Petroleum had seen enough and was ready to pull out. There was just one problem. Its licence required one more well, and walking away meant paying a penalty. The drilling rig had already been hired, so the company decided it might as well use it.

    That autumn, the rig struck a field called Ekofisk, and it was enormous. It sat close to the line Britain and Norway had drawn only four years earlier.

    Norway was suddenly an oil country. But finding oil turned out to be the easy part, because plenty of countries have struck oil and had very little to show for it a generation later. And for a while, it looked as if Norway might become one of them.

    Act 1: who owns the oil1:20 to 2:50

    The obvious explanation is that Norway is rich because it has oil. But Britain had oil too, and in the early years Britain was pumping more of it.

    The first real difference was a decision about who the oil belonged to. Norway had almost no experience of the industry, so some of its best advice came from an unlikely place. A geologist from Iraq called Farouk al-Kasim had moved to Norway in nineteen sixty-eight for family reasonsto check, and he ended up helping to write the plan for how the country would manage its oil. He went on to spend nearly two decades as one of its most senior oil officials.

    The principles Norway settled on were simple. The oil belonged to the Norwegian people, the state would have its own oil company, and the state would take a share of every field.

    It still works that way. When an oil company makes a profit in Norwegian waters today, the tax rate on that profit is seventy-eight percent, and the state owns a direct stake in many of the fields as well.

    Britain started down a similar road. In nineteen seventy-five it created a national oil company of its own. But seven years later the government began selling it off, and by nineteen eighty-eight what was left had been bought by BP.

    So Norway kept more of the money from every barrel. But that's only half the story, because money you collect can still be money you waste.

    By the nineteen eighties, both governments had oil money pouring in. And this is the point where the two countries really went their separate ways.

    Act 2: spend or save3:15 to 5:20

    In Britain, the money went into the ordinary budget. In nineteen eighty-two alone, oil brought in eight billion pounds, which was about eight pounds fifty of every hundred the taxman collected. It helped pay the country's bills and keep other taxes lower than they would have been.

    It's worth being fair about that. Britain had far more people to share its oil between, and it was going through a deep recession with millions out of work. Spending the money was a choice a lot of governments would have made, and some economists still defend it.

    What most people don't know is that, at first, Norway did exactly the same thing. Through the seventies and early eighties it spent its oil money as it arrived, and the economy overheated. When the oil price collapsed in nineteen eighty-six, Norway was hit hardto check.

    That scare is what changed everything. In nineteen ninety, Norway's parliament passed a law creating a fund for the oil money. For six years it sat completely empty, because there was nothing to spare. The first deposit finally arrived in nineteen ninety-six, and it was just under two billion kroner.

    Then came the two rules that matter most. The first is that the money has to be invested outside Norway, in companies and property all over the world, so that it can't flood the economy at home. The second arrived in two thousand and one, and it's the heart of the whole story.

    So how much of its oil fortune is Norway allowed to spend each year? pause

    Around three percent. That's roughly what the fund is expected to earn, after inflation. In other words, Norway doesn't spend its oil money. It spends what the oil money earns.

    In other words, Norway only ever spends what its oil money earns.

    Act 3: the payoff5:20 to 7:00

    Leave money alone like that for thirty years, keep adding to it, and something remarkable happens.

    So how big did the empty fund from nineteen ninety actually get? pause

    At the last count, it was worth around two point three trillion dollars. That works out at roughly four hundred thousand dollars for every single person in Norway.

    It's so large that it owns, on average, about one and a half percent of every company listed on a stock market anywhere in the world. That's more than seven thousand companies. If you own a phone, drive a car or shop on a high street, there's a good chance Norway owns a small piece of the business behind it.

    And it doesn't stop at shares. In twenty eleven, the fund made its very first property investment, and it chose London. It bought a quarter-share in Regent Street from the Crown Estate. So part of one of Britain's most famous streets now earns rent for Norway, paid for with oil from the same sea.

    Because of the three percent rule, that money doesn't run out. This year, transfers from the fund are paying for more than a quarter of the Norwegian government's entire budget, and the fund is still growing.

    So what about Britain? Nobody can know for certain what a British fund would be worth today. Researchers who've tried to work it out have landed anywhere between four hundred and eight hundred and fifty billion pounds. What isn't in doubt is the figure Britain actually has, which is nothing.

    Takeaway7:00 to 7:40

    Norway didn't get rich because it found oil. It got rich because it decided the oil money wasn't income. It was a one-off, and a one-off should be turned into something that lasts.

    That's worth remembering the next time a windfall lands in your own life, whether it's a bonus, an inheritance or a pay rise. You can treat it as extra spending money, and it'll be gone in a year. Or you can do what Norway did, which is to decide on the rule before the money arrives, put it somewhere that grows, and spend only what it earns.
