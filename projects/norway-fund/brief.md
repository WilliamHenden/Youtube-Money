- **The gap to the next fund:** ChatGPT said "roughly $250 billion more than the next largest fund". The real gap is at least $600 billion, because the next largest funds (both in China) are worth about $1.35 to $1.7 trillion. The true figure is the more impressive one.
- **Two phrases left out:** "one of the most powerful investment portfolios ever assembled" and "but perhaps the most remarkable part of this story". Both are grand without saying anything checkable, which is the style listeners recognise as AI writing (`13-script-playbook.md`, section 5). The opening keeps your original "And none of it was supposed to happen". Say if you want either phrase back.
- **"Including almost every major corporation":** softened to "most of the big companies you could name". ChatGPT itself noted the fund doesn't own every major company.
- **"Two thirds" kept** where ChatGPT suggested "more than half". NBIM's own figures at 30 June 2026 give 67%; recheck on publish day.
- **"Here's something remarkable"** (in ChatGPT's suggested Act 3 line) left out, for the same reason as the two phrases above.

## 3. Script draft v3

*`[beat]` marks a deliberate pause of about a second. Numbers are written as they'll be spoken. Every fact is in the fact sheet (section 5).*

### Hook

Norway has the largest sovereign wealth fund in the entire world, currently valued at around two point three trillion dollars. That's at least six hundred billion dollars more than the next largest fund on the planet.

It all started with an unlikely discovery of oil beneath the North Sea. Today, Norway owns a small stake in over seven thousand companies around the world, including most of the big companies you could name.

And none of it was supposed to happen, because Norway's own geologists once told the government there was almost certainly no oil worth finding.

So how did a country of five and a half million people end up with the largest wealth fund on earth? `[beat]`

### Intro sting

*(About three seconds: the channel sting with its musical signature, then the title. The narration picks straight up again.)*

### Context: the last well

The story starts in nineteen fifty-eight, when Norway's government asked its geologists whether there might be anything valuable under the sea off its coast. The reply was blunt. The chances of finding coal, oil or sulphur, they wrote, could be discounted.

For the next ten years, that looked about right. Oil companies came anyway, and between them they drilled more than thirty wells in Norwegian waters without finding anything worth developing.

By nineteen sixty-nine, the American company Phillips Petroleum had had enough, and it was ready to pack up and go home. But its licence still required one more well, and walking away meant paying a penalty. The rig was already hired, so Phillips decided it might as well drill.

That last well struck a field called Ekofisk, and it was enormous. Just before Christmas, Phillips told the Norwegian government what it had found.

So Norway had oil after all. But finding it turned out to be the easy part, because plenty of countries have struck oil and had very little to show for it a generation later. What Norway did next is how a lucky find turned into the biggest fund on earth.

### Act 1: who owns the oil

The first thing Norway had to decide was who the oil belonged to, and it had almost nobody who understood the business. Some of its best help turned up by chance.

In nineteen sixty-eight, an Iraqi geologist called Farouk al-Kasim moved to Norway with his Norwegian wife, so that their youngest son could get the medical care he needed. He'd spent years working in Iraq's oil industry, and with a family to support, he went to the ministry in Oslo looking for work.

They hired him, and he went on to help write the plan for how Norway would run its oil. The principles were simple. The oil belonged to the Norwegian people, the state would have an oil company of its own, and it would keep a share of every field. Parliament agreed ten rules along those lines in nineteen seventy-one, and Norwegians still call them the ten oil commandments.

You can see the result in the tax bill. When an oil company makes a profit in Norwegian waters today, seventy-eight percent of it goes to the state.

So Norway kept most of the money from every barrel.

### Re-hook

Keeping the money still doesn't explain the fund, though, because money you collect can just as easily be money you waste. To see how easily, you only have to look across the water.

### The Britain detour

Britain was sitting on the other half of the same sea. The two countries had split it down the middle, and by the early nineteen eighties Britain's side was producing about three times as much oil as Norway's. So why doesn't Britain have a fund?

It did start down a similar road. In nineteen seventy-five it set up a national oil company of its own. But in nineteen eighty-two, the government turned its oil-producing side into a new company called Britoil and sold it off to investors. Six years later, BP bought it.

The tax money, meanwhile, went into the ordinary budget. In nineteen eighty-two alone, oil brought in close to eight billion pounds, which was about eight pounds in every hundred the taxman collected. It helped pay the country's bills, and it kept other taxes lower than they would have been.

It's only fair to say why. Britain had fourteen times as many people to share its oil between, and it was deep in a recession, with more than three million of them out of work. Plenty of governments would have done the same.

Still, the money was spent as it came in. In twenty eighteen, the think tank IPPR worked out what Britain's oil fund might have been worth if it had saved like Norway. It came to more than five hundred billion pounds. What Britain ended up with was no oil fund at all.

Now, back to Norway, because the awkward truth is that for years it was doing exactly the same thing.

### Act 2: the fright, and the rule

Through the nineteen seventies and into the eighties, Norway spent its oil money as it arrived, and borrowed on top of it. Prices raced ahead and the economy overheated.

Then, in nineteen eighty-six, the price of oil collapsed, and Norway's boom collapsed with it. The years that followed were the bleakest the country had seen since the nineteen thirties, and the state ended up having to rescue its biggest banks.

That fright is where the fund comes from. In nineteen ninety, parliament passed a law to create one, so that oil money would be kept apart from the everyday budget. And then, for six years, the fund sat completely empty, because there was nothing left over to put in it. The first deposit finally arrived in nineteen ninety-six, and it was just under two billion kroner.

Two rules turned that small start into what the fund is today. The first is that the money has to be invested outside Norway, in companies and property all over the world, so that it can't flood the economy at home the way it did before.

The second arrived in two thousand and one, and it's the heart of the whole story. It answers one question. How much of this fortune is Norway allowed to spend each year? `[beat]`

About three percent. That's what the fund is expected to earn each year after inflation, over the long run. It started at four percent and was lowered to three in twenty seventeen. In any one year, Norway can spend a little more or a little less, depending on the economy. But the idea is simple. Over time, Norway spends roughly what the money earns, and leaves the money itself to keep growing for the generations to come.

### Act 3: what the rule built

Leave a rule like that running for thirty years, keep adding oil money on top, and the numbers start to look absurd.

Today, about two thirds of the fund isn't oil money at all. It's what the oil money has earned.

It's now so large that it owns, on average, around one and a half percent of every publicly listed company in the world. If you've bought a phone or filled up a car lately, you've very probably made a little money for Norway. Shared out equally, the fund would come to around four hundred thousand dollars for every person in the country.

It owns property too, and this is where Britain comes back into the story. When the fund made its first ever property purchase, in twenty eleven, it chose London, and it bought a quarter-share of Regent Street. So rent from one of Britain's most famous shopping streets began flowing into Norway's fund, paid for with money first earned from oil beneath the same sea.

And because of the three percent rule, the fund is built to last for generations. This year, it's expected to pay for more than a quarter of Norway's entire national budget.

### The answer, and the takeaway

So how did five and a half million people end up with the largest wealth fund on earth? The oil was only the start. Britain had oil too, and so have dozens of other countries. Norway got there by making three decisions and then sticking to them for decades. It kept hold of the oil money, it put that money where it couldn't easily be spent, and it agreed to live on what the money earned.

Most of us face the same choice on a smaller scale. Sooner or later some money lands in your lap that you weren't counting on, whether it's a bonus or an inheritance. You can treat it as extra spending money, and before long, it'll probably be gone. Or you can do what Norway did, and decide on the rule before the money arrives. Put it somewhere it can grow, and spend only what it earns.

### Outro

America owes more than forty trillion dollars, and it's still the biggest economy on earth. So how does it get away with it? Watch the next video to find out.
