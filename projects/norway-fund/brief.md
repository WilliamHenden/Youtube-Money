## 3. Script draft v2

*`[beat]` marks a deliberate pause of about a second. Numbers are written as they'll be spoken. Every fact is in the fact sheet (section 5).*

### Hook

Every baby born in Norway today arrives with a share, on paper, of around four hundred thousand dollars. So does her mother, and the midwife who delivered her, and every other person in the country.

That's because Norway is sitting on a fund worth two point three trillion dollars. No country on earth has a bigger one, and it owns a small piece of most of the big companies you could name.

And none of it was supposed to happen, because Norway's own geologists once told the government there was no oil to find.

So how did a country of five and a half million people end up with the largest wealth fund on earth? `[beat]`

### Intro sting

*(About three seconds: the channel sting with its musical signature, then the title. The narration picks straight up again.)*

### Context: the last well

The story starts in nineteen fifty-eight, when Norway's government asked its geologists whether there might be anything valuable under the sea off its coast. The reply was blunt. The chances of finding coal, oil or sulphur, they wrote, could be discounted.

For the next ten years, that looked about right. Oil companies came anyway, and between them they drilled more than thirty wells in Norwegian waters without finding anything worth developing.

By nineteen sixty-nine, the American company Phillips Petroleum had had enough, and it was ready to pack up and go home. But its licence still required one more well, and walking away meant paying a penalty. The rig was already hired, so Phillips decided it might as well drill.

That last well struck a field called Ekofisk, and it was enormous. Just before Christmas, Phillips told the Norwegian government what it had found.

So Norway had oil after all. But finding it turned out to be the easy part, because plenty of countries have struck oil and had very little to show for it a generation later. What Norway did next is the reason that baby has her four hundred thousand dollars.

### Act 1: who owns the oil

The first thing Norway had to decide was who the oil belonged to, and it had almost nobody who understood the business. Some of its best help turned up by chance.

In nineteen sixty-eight, an Iraqi geologist called Farouk al-Kasim moved to Norway with his Norwegian wife, so that their youngest son could get the medical care he needed. He'd spent years in Iraq's oil industry and he needed a job, so he went to the ministry in Oslo to look for one.

They hired him, and he went on to help write the plan for how Norway would run its oil. The principles were simple. The oil belonged to the Norwegian people, the state would have an oil company of its own, and it would keep a share of every field. Parliament agreed ten rules along those lines in nineteen seventy-one, and Norwegians still call them the ten oil commandments.

You can see the result in the tax bill. When an oil company makes a profit in Norwegian waters today, seventy-eight percent of it goes to the state.

So Norway kept most of the money from every barrel.

### Re-hook

Keeping the money still doesn't explain the fund, though, because money you collect can just as easily be money you waste. To see how easily, you only have to look across the water.

### The Britain detour

Britain was sitting on the other half of the same sea. The two countries had split it down the middle, and by the early nineteen eighties Britain's side was producing about three times as much oil as Norway's. So why doesn't Britain have a fund?

It did start down a similar road. In nineteen seventy-five it set up a national oil company of its own. Seven years later the government began selling it off, and before the eighties were out, what was left of it belonged to BP.

The tax money, meanwhile, went into the ordinary budget. In nineteen eighty-two alone, oil brought in close to eight billion pounds, which was about eight pounds in every hundred the taxman collected. It helped pay the country's bills, and it kept other taxes lower than they would have been.

It's only fair to say why. Britain had fourteen times as many people to share its oil between, and it was deep in a recession, with more than three million of them out of work. Plenty of governments would have done the same.

Still, the money was spent as it came in. People have since tried to work out what a British oil fund would be worth today, and their answers run anywhere from four hundred to eight hundred and fifty billion pounds. What Britain actually has is no fund at all.

Now, back to Norway, because the awkward truth is that for years it was doing exactly the same thing.

### Act 2: the fright, and the rule

Through the nineteen seventies and into the eighties, Norway spent its oil money as it arrived, and borrowed on top of it. Prices raced ahead and the economy overheated.

Then, in nineteen eighty-six, the price of oil collapsed, and Norway's boom collapsed with it. The years that followed were the bleakest the country had seen since the nineteen thirties, and the state ended up having to rescue its biggest banks.

That fright is where the fund comes from. In nineteen ninety, parliament passed a law to create one, so that oil money would be kept apart from the everyday budget. And then, for six years, the fund sat completely empty, because there was nothing left over to put in it. The first deposit finally arrived in nineteen ninety-six, and it was just under two billion kroner, or about three hundred million dollars.

Two rules turned that small start into what the fund is today. The first is that the money has to be invested outside Norway, in companies and property all over the world, so that it can't flood the economy at home the way it did before.

The second arrived in two thousand and one, and it's the heart of the whole story. It answers one question. How much of this fortune is Norway allowed to spend each year? `[beat]`

About three percent. That's roughly what the fund is expected to earn in a year, after inflation. So the idea is that Norway only ever spends what the money earns, and leaves the money itself alone.

### Act 3: what the rule built

Leave a rule like that running for thirty years, keep adding oil money on top, and the numbers start to look absurd.

At the last count the fund was worth around two point three trillion dollars, and about two thirds of that isn't oil money at all. It's what the oil money has earned.

It's now so large that it owns, on average, about one and a half percent of every company listed on a stock market anywhere in the world. That's more than seven thousand companies. If you've bought a phone or filled up a car lately, you've very probably made a little money for Norway.

It owns property too, and this is where Britain comes back into the story. When the fund made its first ever property purchase, in twenty eleven, it chose London, and it bought a quarter-share of Regent Street. So rent from one of Britain's most famous shopping streets began flowing to Norway, paid for with oil from the same sea.

And because of the three percent rule, the money doesn't run out. This year the fund is paying for more than a quarter of Norway's national budget.

### The answer, and the takeaway

So how did five and a half million people end up with the largest wealth fund on earth? The oil was only the start. Britain had oil too, and so have dozens of other countries. Norway got there by making three decisions and then sticking to them for decades. It kept hold of the oil money, it put that money where it couldn't easily be spent, and it agreed to live on what the money earned.

Most of us face the same choice on a smaller scale. Sooner or later some money lands in your lap that you weren't counting on, whether it's a bonus or an inheritance. You can treat it as spending money, and it'll be gone within the year. Or you can do what Norway did, and decide on the rule before the money arrives. Put it somewhere it can grow, and spend only what it earns.

### Outro

America owes more than forty trillion dollars, and it's still the biggest economy on earth. So how does it get away with it? Watch the next video to find out.

## 4. Notes for the owner

- **Read it aloud, or listen.** Two takes of the hook in the chosen voice are at the link above. Anything that sounds stiff when spoken, mark it and it gets rewritten.
- **The hook can be swapped** for B or C in section 1 without changing anything else.
- **Britain is told fairly on purpose.** It protects the channel from "this is misleading" comments, and it makes Norway's later mistakes more believable.
- **Where the script is careful with the truth:**
  - "No country on earth has a bigger one" means the largest fund owned by a country. It does not say Norway is the richest country, because by income per person it is about fifth to seventh.
  - Regent Street is in the past tense ("began flowing"), because the 2011 purchase is confirmed but I couldn't confirm the fund still holds it today.
  - "About three percent" is today's rule. It was four percent when it was introduced in 2001.
- **Names to check by ear when the narration is made:** Ekofisk (EK-oh-fisk), Farouk al-Kasim (fa-ROOK al-KAH-sim), kroner (KROH-ner).
- **Outro options** (the script uses the first):
  1. "America owes more than forty trillion dollars, and it's still the biggest economy on earth. So how does it get away with it? Watch the next video to find out."
  2. "If you think America owes most of its forty trillion dollars to China, you're in for a surprise. The answer's in the next video."
